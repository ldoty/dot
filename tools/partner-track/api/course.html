Welcome · start here

Make a lot of people wealthy, and pay your investors for it.

This is the grown-up version of Tempi’s course. The shape is the same: explore an idea, check yourself, try it for real, build a skill, write it down. The subject is how to become an investor good enough to run a fund built on Contribution Units, and to keep beating the market while you do it.

Two scoreboards

Every investor is measured on one number: what came back to the people who trusted you with money, net of your fees. You are signing up to be measured on two.

Scoreboard 1 · LPs

Net DPI: cash returned to investors per dollar they paid in, after fees and carry. Measured over ten years and at least two funds. If this number is below the market, the mission ends with the fund.

Scoreboard 2 · People

How many people received at least one year of their own pay from equity in a company you backed. Count them, publish it next to DPI, and report it to LPs every year.

The second number only grows if the first one lets you raise Fund II. Nearly every module comes back to that.

How the course works

Seven modules, named for rounds

Pre-seed through Exit. Each is harder than the last, and the bar at the top fills as you deploy the fund.

One module every week or two

An hour or two of reading and tools, then real work in the world during the week: a model, a memo, a call, a prediction.

Five parts per module

Explore the idea, check yourself, try it with real numbers, practice a skill, and reflect in the journal.

Everything saves

Journal entries, quiz picks, model inputs and predictions save to your account and follow you between devices.

Where you are today · rate yourself 0–10

Be blunt. You’ll rate yourself again at the Exit and compare.

By the end you’ll be able to
  • Read a fund’s terms and say exactly where every dollar goes, from an LP’s commitment to your carry.
  • Explain why venture returns follow a power law and what that does to portfolio size, ownership and reserves.
  • Run a napkin buyout and show what a broad ownership pool costs investors and pays employees.
  • Keep a calibrated record of your own predictions, with a score.
  • Read a term sheet and win a deal a founder could take from someone else.
  • Answer the hardest LP questions about Contribution Units with numbers.
  • Write the Fund I memo you will actually raise on.

A note on the material. The numbers here are typical ranges and teaching models, not legal, tax or investment advice. Fund formation, securities law and adviser registration are work for fund counsel, and the course points to the spots where you will need them.

Pre-seedModule 1 · Explore

How a fund works

A fund is a ten-year promise. Investors commit money, you call it as you need it, and everyone is paid in a fixed order written down before the first check.

Explore · three entities

The fund

A limited partnership (usually Delaware) that owns the investments. Your investors are its limited partners (LPs). It lives about ten years, plus extensions.

The general partner

An LLC you control. It makes the investment decisions and receives the carried interest. Your own commitment to the fund usually flows through it.

The management company

The business that employs you. It receives the management fee and pays salaries, travel, software and rent.

LPs don’t wire their money on day one. They commit it, and you send capital calls as you invest, mostly during a three-to-five year investment period. After that you only support existing companies and wait for exits.

Where the money goes

  • Management fee. Typically 2% of committed capital per year during the investment period, often stepping down afterwards. On $25M that is $500K a year. Over the life of the fund fees usually add up to 15–20% of commitments.
  • Fund expenses. Formation legal work, the fund administrator, the annual audit, tax returns (K-1s) and insurance. Charged to the fund, usually with a cap on formation costs.
  • Carried interest. Typically 20% of profits, paid only after LPs get their contributed capital back. Buyout funds usually add an 8% preferred return (the hurdle) with a catch-up; most venture funds don’t.
  • GP commitment. You invest alongside your LPs, commonly 1–2% of the fund. LPs read it as a measure of how much you believe.

How results are reported

MetricWhat it meansWhy LPs care
TVPITotal value (cash returned + current paper value) ÷ paid-in capitalThe headline, but mostly marks you set yourself
DPICash distributed ÷ paid-in capitalThe only number that can’t be argued with
RVPIRemaining paper value ÷ paid-inTVPI minus DPI: what’s still at risk
IRRAnnualized return, timing includedRewards early exits; easy to flatter with credit lines

Early in a fund, fees and failures arrive before the winners, so returns start negative and climb later. That dip is the J-curve. Every first-time manager lives through it while raising Fund II.

SeedModule 2 · Explore

The power law

In venture, one company can matter more than all the others put together. Everything about how a venture fund is built follows from that.

Explore

Venture outcomes are not a bell curve. Most startups return less than the money put in. A few return 10×. Once in a while one returns 100× or more, and that one pays for the whole fund. Horsley Bridge, a large LP, found that across thirty years of venture investments about 6% of deals produced about 60% of returns.

What follows from it

  • Missing a winner costs more than backing a loser. A failed investment loses 1×. Passing on the company that returns 100× costs you the fund.
  • Ownership times exit size. To return a $25M fund from one company that sells for $1B, you need 2.5% of it at exit, after every later round dilutes you. That usually means 5–10% going in.
  • Reserves. Most seed funds keep 30–50% of the fund to buy more of the companies that are working.
  • Losses aren’t the enemy. The same Horsley Bridge data showed that the best funds often have as many write-offs as average funds. They just also have bigger winners.
  • Consensus is expensive. If every investor agrees a deal is good, the price already says so. Returns come from being right when most people think you’re wrong.
The tension with your mission. Contribution Units in a company that fails are worth nothing. A venture fund makes a lot of people wealthy only through its few big winners. A buyout fund (next module) pays out more often and in smaller amounts. Which one serves Scoreboard 2 better is a real question, and you should answer it before you raise.

Check yourself

Skill builder · Return-the-fund math

Pick five startups you read about this week. For each, write one line: “At a $X entry valuation with $Y check, we own Z%. After three more rounds at 20% dilution each we own W%. To return a $25M fund this company has to sell for $V.” Then ask whether $V is believable. Most of the time it won’t be, and that’s the lesson.

Series AModule 3 · Explore

Buyouts: the other game

Private equity buys control of a business that already works. When you own the company you don’t have to persuade anyone to adopt an ownership pool. You can put one in on the day you close.

Explore

A buyout fund buys a majority of an established, profitable company, usually with a mix of its own equity and borrowed money. Returns come from three places:

Grow earnings

More customers, better pricing, better operations. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) goes up.

Pay down debt

Cash flow repays the loan, so more of the company’s value belongs to the equity at exit.

Sell at a higher multiple

A bigger, cleaner, less owner-dependent business sells for more times its earnings. Don’t count on it.

What $25M buys

Small, profitable private businesses (HVAC, distribution, specialty manufacturing, services) with $1–5M of EBITDA often sell for roughly 4–7× EBITDA. Many are owned by founders nearing retirement with no one to sell to. $25M of equity, with bank or SBA debt, buys three to six of them. There are four common ways in:

  • A committed fund. Raise the $25M first, then buy. LPs need to trust you without seeing the deals.
  • Independent sponsor. Find and sign a deal first, then raise equity for that one deal. The fastest way to build a buyout record.
  • Search fund. Investors pay your salary while you search for one company to buy and then run as CEO.
  • Holding company. Buy businesses with permanent capital and never sell. Good for employees, harder for LP liquidity.

The precedent you’ll cite

KKR has given rank-and-file employees equity in many of its portfolio companies since 2011, an effort led by Pete Stavros. When it sold CHI Overhead Doors in 2022, hourly workers and truck drivers received payouts averaging about $175,000. In 2022 Stavros co-founded Ownership Works, a nonprofit that helps companies and PE firms set up broad-based ownership programs. Teamshares takes another approach: it buys small businesses from retiring owners and moves ownership to employees over time.

Skill builder · Ten listings, ten napkins

Business-for-sale marketplaces list thousands of small companies with their cash flow and asking price. Pick ten. Series BModule 4 · Explore

Judgment you can measure

An investor’s feedback loop is seven to ten years long and full of luck. You can’t learn fast from outcomes, so you learn from your own written predictions.

Explore

A great outcome can come from a bad decision and a good decision can lose money. Annie Duke calls judging decisions by their results “resulting.” Investors who get better over decades protect themselves from it with a written record.

  • Start from the base rate. Before you think about this company, ask how often companies like it succeed. Philip Tetlock’s research on forecasters found that the best ones start with the outside view and then adjust.
  • Write the memo before you decide. What has to be true? What kills it? What’s the return-the-fund math? Why now, and why this team?
  • Put a number on it. “Likely” means 55% to one person and 85% to another. Write “70% chance they raise a Series A within 24 months.”
  • Run a pre-mortem. Imagine it’s three years from now and the investment failed. Write the story of why. Gary Klein’s technique finds risks that optimism hides.
  • Keep an anti-portfolio. Bessemer publishes the famous companies it passed on. Track yours, with the reason you passed.
  • Score yourself. A Brier score measures how close your probabilities were to what happened. 0 is perfect. Always saying 50% scores 0.25.

Try it: Your prediction log

Five a week · resolve them honestly

Write predictions you can check: a startup raising its next round, a public company’s quarter, a deal closing, an exit. Give each a probability and a date. Come back when the date passes and mark what happened.

Check yourself

Skill builder · The one-page memo

Pick a startup from a recent accelerator demo day. Write one page with exactly these headings, then log two predictions about it.

  1. What it is, in one sentence a stranger understands.
  2. What has to be true for it to be worth $1B.
  3. Why now: what changed in the last two years.
  4. Why this team.
  5. Pre-mortem: the most likely way it dies.
  6. Return-the-fund math.
  7. Decision: in or out, and the probability you’d put on being wrong.
Series CModule 5 · Explore

Access, winning and helping

The best companies have a choice of investors. Your judgment is worth nothing if the founder picks someone else.

Explore

Access: get in the room

Be useful before you invest. Publish a point of view so the right founders find you. Earn a reputation with founders who never took your money. Make it easy to get to you.

Winning: get picked

Be fast, clear and honest. Offer clean terms. Have founders vouch for you, including ones whose companies failed. Know their business better than the other investors in the race.

Helping: earn the next deal

Only a few things matter: key hires, the next round, the first big customers, and staying calm in bad board meetings. Do those well and skip the rest.

Your edge here is Contribution Units. A founder who adopts them gets a recruiting pitch nobody else can match: everyone here owns part of what they build, and they keep it if they leave. If you can show it helps them hire and keep people, Contribution Units become a reason to pick you, not a cost of taking your money.

Term sheet basics

TermWhat it does
Pre- and post-moneyValue before and after the new money. Your ownership is check ÷ post-money.
Liquidation preferenceOn a sale, investors get their money back first. Standard is 1× non-participating: the greater of your money back or your share.
Option pool shuffleRequiring a bigger option pool before the money comes in, which dilutes only the founders. Watch for it with the Contribution Unit pool too.
Pro rataThe right to invest in later rounds to keep your ownership. Where reserves get spent.
SAFEY Combinator’s simple agreement for future equity. A post-money SAFE’s cap fixes the investor’s ownership when it converts.
Protective provisionsThings the company can’t do without investor approval, such as selling, raising senior stock, or changing the charter.
Check yourself

Try it: Publish your thesis

One essay · one week
  1. Rewrite your Contribution Units paper as an essay for founders, not investors. Lead with what it does for their hiring.
  2. End with an offer: you’ll model their cap table with and without a pool, for free.
  3. Publish it under your own name. Send it to twenty founders you respect and ask for their honest reaction.
  4. Count the replies, the objections and the founders who ask for the model. That is your first measure of access.
Skill builder · The reference call

Before you invest in a founder, talk to people they didn’t list. Practice on someone you know well with these questions, and take notes.

  1. How do you know them, and for how long?
  2. When things went badly, what did they do in the first week?
  3. Who is the best person they ever hired, and how did they get them?
  4. Would you work for them again? Why or why not?
  5. What should I ask that I haven’t?
GrowthModule 6 · Explore

Ownership that pays

LPs will love the mission and then ask one question: does it cost me returns? You need an answer with numbers in it.

Explore · the case you have to make

Your own model gives the size of the ask. In the Contribution Units paper, investors finish at 62.5% of the company instead of 64.8%. To come out even, the company has to sell for about 3.7% more. Over a ten-year hold that is less than half a point of extra growth a year. The question is whether broad ownership buys that much.

What the evidence says

  • Research on US employee ownership, much of it from Rutgers (Joseph Blasi, Douglas Kruse) and summarized in The Citizen’s Share, generally finds modest productivity gains and lower turnover and layoffs. The gains are strongest when ownership comes with real information and a voice in how work is done.
  • KKR’s portfolio companies with broad ownership have reported lower turnover and better safety and engagement. Treat company-reported numbers as encouraging, not proof.
  • No one has a clean, long-run study of an all-employee pool in venture-backed startups. That gap is your opportunity: you could be the one who measures it.

What could go wrong

  • Dilution. If the pool isn’t refilled at each round, it shrinks to nothing by Series C. Your paper handles this; your term sheets have to enforce it.
  • Tax and securities rules. Units in an LLC, restricted stock or RSUs in a corporation, Rule 701 for compensatory grants, 409A valuations. The structure differs by company type. This is work for counsel, early.
  • People don’t value what they don’t understand. Equity nobody explains does nothing for retention. Budget for statements and education.
  • Mission drift. In a bad year, the pool is the easiest thing to cut. Write it into your fund’s documents so you can’t.
Measure it from deal one. For every portfolio company, record turnover, time to fill roles, offer acceptance rate and engagement, before and after. In five years you will have the only dataset on this, and Fund II’s pitch writes itself.
Check yourself

Try it: Steelman the skeptic

Three objections · three answers

Write the three strongest objections a smart, skeptical LP would raise, in their words, not yours. Then answer each with a number, a precedent, or a mechanism you will build. If you can’t answer one, that’s the gap to close before you raise.

Skill builder · Talk to the people on Scoreboard 2

Find three people who got a meaningful equity payout and three who held options that ended up worthless or were too expensive to exercise. Ask what they understood about their equity when they joined, whether it changed how they worked, and what they did with the money. Their answers belong in your pitch.

Beyond the modules

The plan: the company, then the firm

You and your brother start a company that runs Contribution Units from the first hire. You publish the template and talk about it. Once the company can run without you, you leave to run the firm and raise Fund I on what the company proved.

Why this order

The company is the proof

“We run it ourselves, and here is what happened to hiring and turnover” is the evidence LPs ask for in Module 6. You’ll have it before you raise.

The template is the access

Founders who adopt it already agree with you and know your name. That is the deal flow problem from Module 5, partly solved.

LPs back operators

A founder who turned into an investor, with a thesis that came out of their own company, is a story LPs know how to fund.

Year 0

Found it right

  • Put Contribution Units in the cap table and founders’ agreement before the first hire.
  • Have counsel settle the structure: C-corp or LLC, real units or a share of sale proceeds (Module 6). Whatever they build for you becomes the template.
  • Write the split terms now, while everyone gets along (the checklist below).
Years 0–2

Build, measure, publish

  • Measure from employee one: turnover, time to fill roles, offer acceptance, and whether people understand their units.
  • Publish the template once your own version has survived counsel. Write the essay for founders; give the talks.
  • On the side: small angel checks, five predictions a week, a memo for every deal you see.
Years 2–4

Prove it spreads

  • Count the startups that adopt the template, and keep in touch with each.
  • Lead a few SPVs (single-deal vehicles) or one deal-by-deal buyout. Everyone who invests with you is a future LP.
  • The company reaches the split triggers you wrote down in year 0.
The split

Hand it over

Your brother or a hired CEO runs the company. You keep what the agreement says, and you start the firm.

Years 4–6

Raise Fund I

Pitch on three things: the company’s data, the companies that adopted the template, and your angel and SPV record. Hire fund counsel, find an anchor for 10–20%, hold a first close around 40–60% of target and a final close within about a year.

Years 6–20

Become very good

The best investors are judged on three funds, not one. Fund II is raised on Fund I’s early marks and your Scoreboard 2 data. Fund III is raised on cash returned.

The split · write it down in year 0
  • What triggers it. A revenue level, a Series A, or a CEO in place who isn’t you. Something you can both check.
  • Your equity when you leave. How much has vested, what keeps vesting, and whether you keep a board seat.
  • The firm investing in the company. Allowed, with your fund’s LP advisory committee approving the conflict.
  • Who owns the template and the name. The company, you, or an open license nobody owns.
  • Conflicts. A written policy for when the firm invests in the company’s customers or competitors.
An idea to test · the product is the tooling

The company could sell what makes Contribution Units easy: plan documents, a ledger that runs off payroll, statements for employees, and handling the pool at a sale. Then the company, the template and the talks are one thing. Every customer is a startup that adopted Contribution Units, which becomes the firm’s deal flow, and the company’s data is the evidence. Test whether the market is big enough to build a real company. If it isn’t, build the company you two are best placed to win and run Contribution Units inside it.
