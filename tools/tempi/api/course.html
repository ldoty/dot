Welcome · start here

Helping kids do the things kids do.

Hi Tempi. A pediatric occupational therapist (OT) helps children take part in their everyday lives: playing, learning, getting dressed, eating, making friends, and handling a loud and busy world. This course lets you look at the job from the inside and practice the skills OTs use every day. You can start practicing them at 13.

How the course works

Six modules, soft to firm

Therapy putty comes in colors that tell you how hard it is to squeeze, from tan (softest) to black (firmest). The modules follow the same colors. Module 1 is the easiest and the capstone is the hardest.

One module a week

Each module takes about an hour to read and work through, plus a hands-on activity you do during the week. Six weeks total, or go faster.

Five parts per module

Explore the idea, check yourself, try it in real life, practice a skill, and reflect in your journal.

Your journal stays with you

What you write saves automatically in this browser on this device. The Journal page collects it all and lets you copy it out.

By the end you'll be able to
  • Explain what OT is (and how it's different from physical and speech therapy) to anyone who asks.
  • Describe what a workday looks like in a school, a clinic, a hospital and a family's living room.
  • Recognize major milestones in how children's hands and bodies develop.
  • Name all eight senses, including the three most people have never heard of.
  • Break any activity into parts and make it easier or harder on purpose.
  • Write an observation, a goal and a play-based session plan for a practice client.
  • Map the exact steps from where you are now to becoming a licensed OT.

A note on the activities. Anything marked with a grown-up means you'll need a parent's help, for example to contact a real therapist or to play with a younger kid. Real OTs always work with families and never alone with someone else's child without permission. You'll practice that habit from the start.

What OTs actually do

In OT, an "occupation" means anything you need or want to do with your day. For a kid, playing, learning, eating, dressing, sleeping and making friends are all occupations.

Explore

When most people hear "occupation" they think of a job. OTs use the word more broadly. A child's occupations are the everyday activities that fill their life. A pediatric OT steps in when something makes those activities hard. That could be a diagnosis like autism, ADHD, cerebral palsy or Down syndrome, an early birth, an injury, or a hand that tires out too fast to finish a worksheet.

OTs don't ask "what's wrong with this child?" They ask "what does this child want and need to do, and what's getting in the way?" Then they can help in three different places:

The person

Build the skill itself: strength, coordination, balance, attention, or calming down when overwhelmed.

The activity

Change how it's done: Velcro shoes instead of laces, a thicker pencil grip, steps written on a picture card.

The environment

Change the surroundings: a quieter seat at lunch, a slanted writing board, noise-reducing headphones.

OTs call this the Person–Environment–Occupation idea: how well someone can do something depends on all three working together. A great OT can usually find a fix in at least one of them.

Origin story. OT became an official profession in the U.S. in 1917. Early OTs used crafts, gardening and meaningful work to help soldiers coming home from World War I and patients in hospitals rebuild their lives. The core belief hasn't changed: doing things that matter to you helps you heal and grow.

Check yourself: who would you call?

OTs work alongside physical therapists (PTs) and speech-language pathologists (SLPs). The lines blur, but each has a home base. Pick who you'd call first.

Try it: Your occupation log

One school day · 20 min total
  1. From waking up to falling asleep, jot down everything you do. Small things count: brushing teeth, unzipping a backpack, texting, opening a yogurt.
  2. Next to each, mark H if it needs fine hand skills, P if it needs planning or remembering steps, S if sounds, textures or movement are part of it.
  3. Pick three activities and try them using only your non-dominant hand. Notice what you do differently.
  4. Count: how many of your day's occupations would be harder if your hands, senses or planning didn't cooperate?
Skill builder · Asking the OT question

OT starts with curiosity about the person, not the problem. The first thing an OT makes is an "occupational profile": who is this person, what do they love, what's hard, and what do they hope for? Practice on someone you know. Ask a family member these three questions and listen without fixing anything:

  1. What does a normal day look like for you?
  2. What's something you love doing that you wish you had more time for?
  3. What's one everyday thing that's more annoying or harder than it should be?

A day in the life

"Pediatric OT" covers several different jobs. The same training can lead you to a school hallway, a gym full of swings, a hospital nursery, or a family's kitchen floor.

Explore four workplaces

Other places pediatric OTs work: private practices they own, telehealth (sessions over video), summer camps, horse-riding therapy programs, adaptive sports leagues, children's mental health programs, and universities where they teach and do research.

Check yourself: which setting fits you?

There's no wrong answer. This helps you notice your own preferences.

Try it: Watch like an OT

This week · 45 min
  1. with a grown-up Search YouTube for "pediatric occupational therapy session" and pick two videos from different settings (a clinic and a school, for example).
  2. Watch once just for fun. Then watch again with the sound off and track only what the therapist does: where they stand, how they set up materials, how they help without taking over.
  3. Write down three things the OT did that a regular babysitter probably wouldn't.
Skill builder · Explaining it in 30 seconds

Pediatric OTs explain their work all the time, to parents, teachers, doctors and kids. Practice an "elevator pitch." Write a 2–3 sentence explanation of pediatric OT that a 7-year-old would understand, then a version for a doctor. Read both out loud and time them.

How kids grow (especially their hands)

Skills build in a predictable order. To know whether a child needs help, an OT has to know what's typical for each age.

Explore

A newborn's hands mostly stay fisted. Within about six years, that same child can button a shirt, cut along a line and write their name. This happens in a fairly predictable sequence, and OTs memorize it. The ages are always ranges, not deadlines. Kids vary a lot, and being a little "late" on one skill usually means nothing on its own.

One of the most useful ideas in pediatric OT is "stability before mobility." Fine finger control depends on a steady trunk and shoulder. That's why an OT might have a child with messy handwriting crawl through tunnels, do wheelbarrow walks or color while lying on their stomach. It looks like play, but it builds the stable base the fingers work from.

Gross motor

Big movements: sitting, crawling, walking, jumping, catching a ball.

Fine motor

Small, precise hand movements: grasping, pinching, buttoning, writing.

Visual-motor

Eyes and hands working together, like copying a shape or tracing a line.

Self-care (ADLs)

"Activities of daily living": dressing, eating, toileting, bathing, grooming.

    Try it: The grip lab

    20 min · paper, pencil, winter gloves
    1. Write your full name five times: (a) normally, (b) holding the pencil in your fist like a toddler, (c) with your non-dominant hand, (d) wearing thick winter gloves, (e) holding the pencil at the very top of its eraser end.
    2. Rate each one from 1 (easy) to 5 (exhausting), and time it.
    3. Notice what your body did to cope. Did you press harder? Tense your shoulder? Lean in closer? Slow way down?
    4. Those workarounds have a name: compensations. OTs watch for them in kids because they tell you where the real difficulty is.
    Skill builder · Know the science

    OT school starts with anatomy. Get a head start on the hand:

    • Each hand and wrist has 27 bones: 8 carpals (wrist), 5 metacarpals (palm) and 14 phalanges (fingers).
    • The fleshy pad at the base of your thumb is the thenar eminence. Its muscles let your thumb touch each fingertip, called opposition. Very few other animals can do this.
    • Most writing grips mature into a dynamic tripod: the pencil rests against the middle finger and is pinched by the thumb and index finger, with the fingers doing the moving instead of the whole arm.

    Challenge: without looking, touch your thumb to each fingertip in order and back again, ten times fast. Now try it on your other hand.

    Your eight senses

    You learned about five senses in kindergarten. OTs work with eight, and the three extra ones explain a lot of kids' behavior.

    Explore

    The famous five

    Sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell.

    Vestibular

    Balance and movement, sensed in your inner ear. It tells you whether you're upright, spinning or speeding up.

    Proprioception

    Body position, sensed by your muscles and joints. It's how you can touch your nose with your eyes closed.

    Interoception

    Signals from inside your body: hunger, thirst, needing the bathroom, a pounding heart, butterflies.

    Your brain constantly turns the "volume" of each sense up or down. That's called sensory processing. Some kids' volume is set very high: a shirt tag feels like sandpaper, and a fire drill is physically painful. Others have it set low: they don't notice their name being called or food on their face. Some kids seek out more input, so they crash, spin, chew and climb everything.

    None of these are bad behavior. They're a nervous system trying to get comfortable. A big OT tool is "heavy work": pushing, pulling or carrying things. It gives the muscles and joints strong input, and that calms many kids. That's why an OT might ask a restless student to carry books to the library.

    Think like a scientist. Much of this work traces back to Dr. A. Jean Ayres, an OT and psychologist who developed sensory integration theory in the 1960s and 70s. Research on it is still developing. Some approaches have solid evidence and others are debated. Good OTs read the research and measure whether what they're doing actually helps each child.

    Check yourself: which sense is it?

    Try it: Map your own sensory profile

    Everyone has a sensory profile, including you. Slide each sense toward what's true for you. There's no right answer.

    Try it: Three-day sensory notice

    3 days · 5 min each evening
    1. Each evening, write down one thing that bugged your senses that day (a sound, texture, smell or crowd).
    2. Write one thing that helped you feel calm or focused. Was it movement, quiet, music, a snack, a hoodie?
    3. After three days, look for a pattern. What's your best "reset" when you're overwhelmed?
    Skill builder · Curiosity over judgment

    When a kid "misbehaves," an OT asks what that behavior is doing for them. Rewrite each of these from a judgment into a curious question:

    • "He won't sit still in circle time." → What might his body be looking for when he moves?
    • "She's such a picky eater."
    • "He's always crashing into people at recess."
    Blue · firmModule 5 · Explore

    Break it down, play it up

    OTs have two core skills: taking any activity apart to see what it really demands, and turning hard practice into a game a kid wants to play.

    Explore: activity analysis

    To you, brushing your teeth is one thing. To an OT, it's more than a dozen steps, and each one needs something different. Open the cap (two hands doing different jobs). Squeeze the right amount (controlled grip strength). Brush every surface (planning and sequencing). Tolerate the minty foam (sensory). Remember to do it at all (routine and memory). When a child struggles, the OT looks for the one step that's breaking down.

    Once they find it, they grade the activity, making it a little easier or harder by changing one thing at a time. The target is the "just-right challenge": hard enough that the child grows, easy enough that they succeed most of the time. Too easy is boring. Too hard leads to frustration and kids giving up.

    Check yourself: the grading machine

    Your client: Leo, age 4. He can open and close scissors to snip paper but can't yet cut along a line. Adjust the activity until it lands in Leo's just-right zone.

    Explore: play is the treatment

    A 5-year-old won't do fifty finger-pinch exercises. They'll happily pinch fifty clothespins to "rescue" toy animals trapped in a jail made of string. OTs are experts at hiding therapy inside play:

    The gameWhat it secretly builds
    Picking up pom-poms with tongs or tweezersPincer grasp, hand strength, eye-hand coordination
    Digging beads out of therapy puttyFinger strength and endurance for writing
    Animal walks: bear, crab, wheelbarrowShoulder and core stability (stability before mobility)
    Obstacle coursesMotor planning: figuring out how to move your body through something new
    Writing letters in shaving creamLetter formation, plus touch tolerance for kids who avoid messy textures
    Coloring on paper taped to a wallWrist position for writing, shoulder strength

    Try it: Build a three-station obstacle course

    This week · 1 hour · with a grown-up
    1. Find a younger kid around 4–7 to play with, such as a cousin, neighbor or family friend, with their parent's OK.
    2. Design three stations, each secretly targeting one skill: balance, hand strength, and motor planning. Use household stuff like pillows, a broom handle across two books, a laundry basket, or clothespins.
    3. Give it a story ("cross the lava river, rescue the stuffed dog, climb the mountain").
    4. Run it and watch. Where did they struggle? Where did they get bored? Change one thing and run it again. That's grading in real life.
    Skill builder · Task analysis

    Break tying shoes into every single step, as if you were writing instructions for a robot. Most people list 5. OTs usually find 12 or more. Then circle the step you think is hardest for a 6-year-old, and invent one way to grade it easier.

    Capstone: Meet Maya

    Now you'll do what an OT does with a new client. Observe carefully, describe what you saw, set a goal, and plan a session.

    Referral · Maya, age 6, first gradePractice case (fictional)

    From her teacher

    Avoids writing and often finishes 4 of 10 spelling words. Holds her pencil in a fist with her thumb wrapped over. Letters are large and run off the lines. Covers her ears at lunch and hides under a table during assemblies.

    From her parents

    Mornings take forever. She refuses socks because "the bumps hurt," and getting dressed ends in tears. "We want her to like school and get out the door without a fight."

    What Maya loves

    Horses (she draws them constantly with markers), her dog Biscuit, swinging at the park, and being the line leader.

    Why Maya is made up. Real OTs follow privacy laws (HIPAA in health care, FERPA in schools). They never share a child's name or details outside the care team. So you practice on fictional cases, just like OT students do.

    Step 1 · Observation or interpretation?

    An observation is something anyone in the room would agree they saw or measured. An interpretation is a guess about what it means. OTs write observations first and say clearly when they're interpreting.

    Step 2 · Write a goal

    OT goals have a recipe: By when, who will do what, how well, with what help. Here's an example:

    By the end of the school year, Maya will copy 5 spelling words legibly within the lines, using a pencil grip and a slanted writing board, with no more than one reminder from her teacher.

    Now write one goal for Maya's mornings, using what her parents said matters to them.

    Skill builder · The SOAP note

    After every session, OTs write a short note in a format called SOAP:

    • S · Subjective: what the child or family said. "Maya said, 'I want to make a horse!'"
    • O · Objective: what you saw and measured. "Picked up 12 of 20 pom-poms with tongs in 3 minutes."
    • A · Assessment: what you think it means. "Grip strength improving; tired after 2 minutes."
    • P · Plan: what's next. "Add putty warm-up; try short pencil next session."

    Imagine you just ran your session. Write a SOAP note for it below.

    Language matters. You'll hear "child with autism" (person-first) and "autistic child" (identity-first). Many autistic people prefer identity-first, and many people with other disabilities prefer person-first. The respectful move is the OT move: ask the person or family what they prefer, and use it.

    Beyond the modules · Prepare

    The path: from 13 to OTR

    "OTR" stands for Occupational Therapist, Registered. It's the credential you earn after grad school and a national exam. Here's the full route in the United States, starting where you are now.

    1. 13–14
      Now

      Explore and notice

      Finish this course. Read the books in the Field Kit. Notice the kids around you and how they move, play and cope. Babysit if you can, since time with little kids is good experience.

    2. 14–18
      High school

      Build the base

      • Classes: biology, chemistry, anatomy & physiology if offered, psychology (AP Psych is great), statistics, and lots of writing. Spanish or another language is a real asset in many communities.
      • Volunteer with kids with disabilities: adaptive sports, Special Olympics, inclusive camps, buddy programs, library story time, tutoring.
      • Get certified in CPR and first aid.
      • with a grown-up Ask to shadow a pediatric OT for a day.
    3. 18–22
      College

      Bachelor's degree, any major

      Common majors are kinesiology, psychology, health science, exercise science and human development. What matters most is finishing the prerequisites most OT programs want: anatomy & physiology I and II with labs, intro psychology, developmental (lifespan) psychology, abnormal psychology, statistics, sociology, and often medical terminology.

      Collect observation hours with OTs in different settings, since many programs require them. Keep a strong GPA. You apply through a shared application called OTCAS. Some universities offer combined bachelor's-plus-master's programs that save a year.

    4. 22–25
      Grad school

      Master's (about 2–2.5 years) or doctorate (about 3 years)

      Both an entry-level master's (MOT/MSOT) and an entry-level doctorate (OTD) qualify you to practice. You'll study anatomy (often with a cadaver lab), neuroscience, development, mental health and treatment methods.

      Fieldwork is where you train with real clients. Level I placements are short. Level II is at least 24 weeks full-time, supervised by a licensed OT. Doctoral students also complete a 14-week capstone project.

    5. ~25
      Licensed

      Pass the NBCOT exam, then get a state license

      Passing the national exam from NBCOT (the National Board for Certification in Occupational Therapy) earns you "OTR." Then you apply for a license in the state where you'll work.

    6. 25+
      Career

      Grow into a specialist

      Start in a school, clinic, early intervention program or hospital. With experience you can earn AOTA's Board Certification in Pediatrics or get advanced training in feeding, the NICU, sensory integration, assistive technology or hippotherapy (therapy using horses). Later you might supervise, open a practice, teach or do research.

    The numbers

    About $96,000

    Median yearly pay for U.S. occupational therapists in May 2023 (Bureau of Labor Statistics). Pediatric and school roles often pay somewhat less than hospitals.

    Faster than average

    The BLS projects OT jobs will grow faster than the average for all jobs over the next decade.

    6–7 years after high school

    4 years of college plus 2–3 of grad school. Grad tuition varies widely, from tens of thousands of dollars to more than $100,000, so scholarships and in-state programs matter.

    Numbers change. Check the latest at the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook.

    A shorter route: the OT assistant. An occupational therapy assistant (OTA) earns about a two-year associate degree, passes their own national exam, and carries out treatment plans under an OT's supervision. Some people work as an OTA first and later go back to become an OT.

    Interview a real pediatric OT

    with a grown-up Most OTs love talking to students. Ask your pediatrician's office, your school, or a local children's therapy clinic. Take notes in your journal.

    5. Tell me about a kid whose progress you'll never forget (without names).
    6. How much of your day is paperwork and documentation?

    8. Which settings have you worked in, and which did you like best?

    Glossary
    ADLs
    Activities of daily living: basic self-care like dressing, eating, bathing and toileting.
    IADLs
    Instrumental ADLs: more complex tasks like cooking, managing money and using a phone.
    Fine motor
    Small, precise movements of the hands and fingers.
    Gross motor
    Large movements of the whole body: walking, jumping, climbing.
    Visual-motor integration
    Coordinating what the eyes see with what the hands do.
    Praxis / motor planning
    Figuring out how to do a new movement, then sequencing and carrying it out.
    Bilateral coordination
    Using both sides of the body together, often doing different jobs (one hand holds, one cuts).
    Crossing midline
    Reaching across the center of your body, like your right hand to your left side.
    Vestibular
    The sense of balance and movement, sensed in the inner ear.
    Proprioception
    The sense of where your body parts are, sensed in muscles and joints.
    Interoception
    The sense of internal body signals like hunger, thirst and heartbeat.
    Heavy work
    Pushing, pulling or carrying that gives strong proprioceptive input; often calming.
    Grading
    Adjusting an activity to be easier or harder by changing one part of it.
    Just-right challenge
    An activity hard enough to grow from, easy enough to succeed at.
    Occupational profile
    The OT's first picture of a client: their history, interests, routines and goals.
    IEP
    Individualized Education Program: a legal plan for a student who gets special education services, often including OT.
    Early intervention
    Services for babies and toddlers (birth to age 3) with developmental delays, usually at home.
    SOAP note
    A note format: Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan.
    Fieldwork
    Supervised hands-on training with real clients during OT school.
    OTR / OTA
    Registered occupational therapist / occupational therapy assistant.
